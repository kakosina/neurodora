Anatomy of a chorus

Still in your head tomorrow.

If a chorus only makes sense with the verse, it might be an excellent pre-chorus.

A good chorus rarely looks impressive on paper. It might have eight words, half of them repeated, with a rhyme you can practically see coming. Then it lives in your head for a week.

One thought, please

A verse can change direction. A chorus benefits from being a little dumber. It picks one reaction and sticks with it to the end.

overload
In four lines, the narrator manages to forgive, accuse, remember summer, declare independence, and want to come back.
focus
The whole chorus revolves around one thing: “don’t text me — I’ll reply anyway.”

A chorus needs a shape

Not necessarily four neat lines. But listeners should know where they’ve landed. A repeated word, a return to the first phrase, the same melodic gesture: they all create an outline.

A / A

One phrase repeats with a small change.

A / B / A

The beginning returns at the end.

Staircase

Each line turns up the pressure a little.

Cutoff

The phrase ends sooner than you expect.

The words need to stand up to repetition

A complex metaphor can impress you once. By the fourth repeat, it’s demanding your attention. That’s why short verbs, pronouns, and concrete words suit a chorus.

You can admire a verse. A chorus makes you want to scream.

Five checks

01
Without the music

Can you remember the main phrase ten minutes later?

02
In one gesture

Does the chorus have a single melodic shape?

03
Without explanation

Does the emotion make sense without the second verse?

04
The third time around

Is the main line starting to get on your nerves?

05
At a show

Is there a part the crowd will pick up on its own?

in brief

The idea in a chorus is usually simpler than the one in a verse. That’s no concession on quality. It’s the hardest-earned simplicity in the song.
