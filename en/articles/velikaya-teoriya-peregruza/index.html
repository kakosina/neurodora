The grand theory of distortion

Distortion increases the scale

In cute rock, the guitar isn’t there to prove it belongs to rock. It has a more useful job: changing the size of an event.

An “ok” message is small on its own. But if the heroine spent all day waiting for a different answer, it’s no longer a small event. Distortion is there so the music takes her side.

Cute rock takes an event the size of a notification and turns it into a stadium.

The funnier the reason, the more the sound matters

Cute rock has strange mathematics.

A major tragedy doesn’t always need a big arrangement. Sometimes it’s already clear enough. But a tiny everyday catastrophe needs amplification.

Didn’t hit like. Deleted the photo. Replied six hours later. Called her “bro.”

To an outside observer, it’s garbage. To a song, it’s a ready-made emergency.

The first law of distortion: if the guitar changes nothing about the scene’s emotional scale, you can switch it off.

The law of the emotional coefficient

The emotional coefficient at work: the smaller an event is to humanity and the bigger it is to one person, the greater its cute rock potential.

The end of the world for everyone: 2 points. The end of the world because he texted “got it”: 100 points.

Zero scientific value. A surprising amount of practical value.

The guitar in cute rock isn’t decoration. It’s magnification.
